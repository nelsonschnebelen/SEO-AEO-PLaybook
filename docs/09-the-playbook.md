# The playbook — all twelve chapters

The full method, ordered by return on effort. The site shows the short version of each chapter;
this is the whole thing.

## 1. The first afternoon

If you do nothing else in this playbook, do these five things. For a typical independent restaurant they account for the large majority of the available upside, and none of them requires a developer, an agency or a budget.

- **Claim and verify your Google Business Profile.** Free. Twenty minutes plus verification time. Everything else in local search depends on it.

- **Fix your hours, including holidays for the next twelve months.** Wrong hours are the single most expensive small error in this industry — a wasted trip becomes a one-star review that outlives the mistake by years.

- **Set the most specific accurate primary category.** Thirty seconds of work. One of the strongest ranking levers you have.

- **Get your menu out of the PDF and onto a real web page.** The highest-value hour of work most restaurants can do. Covered fully in chapter 3.

- **Ask every guest for a Google review, starting tonight.** Not a campaign — a habit, built into the check drop.

> **Before you buy anything**
>
> If someone is selling you "SEO services" for a restaurant and has not first asked to see your Google Business Profile, they are not doing local SEO. Do this list yourself first. Then, if you hire help, you will actually be able to tell whether they are adding anything.

## 2. Google Business Profile, properly

For most restaurants the profile out-earns the website. It is where the calls, the direction taps and the "open now" decisions happen. Treat it as your primary storefront, not an afterthought.

### How Google decides who shows up

Google describes local ranking as three factors. In practice they behave like this:

| Factor | What it means | What you can do |
|---|---|---|
| Relevance | How well your profile matches what was searched. | A lot. Categories, attributes, menu items, description, services, review language. |
| Distance | How far you are from the searcher or the place they named. | Nothing directly — but relevance and prominence widen the radius you can win in. |
| Prominence | How well known and well regarded you are, online and off. | A lot, slowly. Reviews, local press, links, citations, brand searches. |

You cannot move your building. So the whole game is relevance (fast to fix) and prominence (slow to build, compounding). Start with relevance.

### Categories: the highest-leverage field on the profile

Your **primary category** carries far more weight than the secondaries. The instinct is to pick something broad so you show up for more — that is backwards. "Restaurant" puts you in a pool with every restaurant in the city. "Neapolitan pizza restaurant" puts you in a pool of maybe four, and Google still shows you for broader pizza searches because it understands the relationship.

- Pick the **single most specific accurate** primary category.

- Add two to five secondaries for genuine secondary services: Caterer, Bar, Breakfast restaurant, Takeout restaurant, Event venue.

- Do **not** add categories for things you do not do. It dilutes relevance and can trigger a suspension.

- Re-check annually — Google adds and retires categories, and a better one may now exist.

### The fields nearly everyone leaves blank

These are free, take minutes, and directly determine which filtered searches and AI questions you are eligible for:

- **Attributes** — outdoor seating, delivery, takeout, dine-in, wheelchair accessible entrance and restroom, gender-neutral restroom, dog friendly, good for kids, good for groups, LGBTQ+ friendly, free wifi, parking type, payment methods.

- **Dietary options** — vegetarian, vegan, gluten-free, halal, kosher. These power a large volume of high-intent searches with very little competition.

- **More hours** — separate kitchen, brunch, happy hour, delivery, takeout and senior-hours windows.

- **Services and offerings** — reservations, private dining, catering, curbside, no-contact delivery, live music, sports on TV.

- **Opening date** — helps with entity confidence and shows longevity.

- **Menu, order and reserve links** — pointed at pages *you* control.

> **Watch your ordering links**
>
> Third-party delivery platforms can attach their own ordering links to your profile without asking. Every tap through one of those is a commission on an order that was already yours. Check your profile on a phone monthly, tap every button, and set your own ordering provider as the preferred one wherever Google allows it.

### Photos: freshness beats production value

Profiles with regular new photos get materially more engagement, and a profile whose last photo is three years old reads as closed. A phone camera in good light is completely sufficient. What matters is coverage and cadence.

- **Cover the set:** exterior with signage in daylight, exterior at night, the entrance, the dining room full, the dining room empty, the bar, the patio, parking, 10 or more hero dishes, the team, the kitchen.

- **Shoot dishes from the diner's angle**, in natural light where you can, without a filter that changes what the food actually looks like.

- **Upload a few every week** rather than forty at once. Steady beats spiky.

- **Add a cover photo and a logo**, and check them on a phone — Google crops hard.

### Posts, Q&A and messaging

Posts put your specials directly into search results and signal an active business — aim for two to four a month, always with a photo and a button. The Questions & Answers section deserves more attention than it gets: **anyone** can answer a question about your business, including people who are wrong or a competitor. Seed it yourself with the ten questions you actually get asked, answer them as the owner, and check it monthly. Those owner answers get quoted by AI assistants unusually often.

## 3. The menu problem

This is the most common expensive mistake in the industry, so it gets its own chapter.

If your menu is a PDF, a JPEG, a Flash-era slideshow or an image of a chalkboard, then every dish name, every price, every allergen note and every description on it is **invisible**. Google cannot index it reliably. AI assistants cannot read it. Someone searching for the exact dish you are known for will not find you, because as far as the machines are concerned, you do not serve it.

> **The test**
>
> Open your menu on your website and try to select a dish name with your cursor or by long-pressing on a phone. If you cannot select it as text, no search engine and no AI assistant can read it either.

### What a menu page should be

- **Real HTML text** — dish names, descriptions and prices as selectable text on a normal web page.

- **Structured with headings** — each menu section as its own heading, so both readers and machines can see the shape of it.

- **Described, not just listed** — one or two sentences per dish covering the main ingredients and the cooking method. This is what matches specific searches.

- **Tagged for diet** — vegetarian, vegan, gluten-free, contains nuts. High-intent, low-competition, and genuinely useful.

- **Priced** — visible prices reduce bounce and feed price-range signals.

- **Current** — an outdated menu is worse than no menu. Update it the day the kitchen changes.

- **Marked up with schema** — see chapter 7.

Keep the PDF as a download if your regulars like printing it. But the HTML page is the real menu, and it should be the one your Google profile links to.

> **🤖 The AI shortcut**
>
> Photograph each page of your printed menu, hand the images to an AI assistant, and ask it to output clean HTML with sections, dish names, descriptions, prices and dietary tags. What used to be an afternoon of typing becomes about fifteen minutes. Then check every price yourself — this is exactly the kind of detail AI gets subtly wrong, and a wrong price on your website is a fight at the table. The prompt is in the prompt library.

## 4. A website that does its job

Your website does not need to win awards. It needs to load fast on a phone with two bars of signal and let a hungry person do one of four things immediately: see the menu, get directions, call you, or book a table.

### The non-negotiables

| Requirement | Why | How to check |
|---|---|---|
| Loads in under 3 seconds on mobile | Most of your traffic is on a phone, often in motion and impatient. | pagespeed.web.dev, or load it on your phone with wifi off |
| HTTPS with no warnings | Warning screens kill conversion outright. | Padlock in the address bar |
| Address, phone, hours as text on every page | Machines cannot read an image of your phone number. | Try to select the text with your cursor |
| Tap-to-call phone number | A phone number that is not a link costs you calls. | Tap it on your phone |
| Order and Reserve above the fold | Traffic that cannot convert is a cost. | Count the taps from homepage to cart |
| Unique title tag per page, with your city | Strongest single on-page signal. | Compare your browser tab text across pages |
| In Google's index | If it is not indexed it does not exist. | Search site:yourdomain.com |
| Search Console connected | It is the only free view of what people searched before clicking. | search.google.com/search-console |

### The pages that earn their keep

In rough order of value for a single-location restaurant:

- **Homepage** — who you are, where you are, hours, and the four buttons.

- **Menu** — HTML, described, priced, current.

- **Visit / Location** — address, map, parking, transit, landmarks, accessibility, the practical facts. This page does double duty for AEO.

- **FAQ** — the highest-leverage AEO page you can build. Chapter 8.

- **About** — the chef, the story, the sourcing, real names. Credibility signals.

- **Private events / catering** — if you do it, this is often the highest revenue per visitor page on the whole site.

- **Events / specials** — freshness, and it captures "what's on tonight" searches.

Notice what is not on that list: a blog nobody reads, an image slider, an intro animation, a background video that eats 8MB before the menu renders, and a popup that covers the phone number.

### Title tags that work for restaurants

The pattern that performs, roughly 55 characters or fewer:

- Homepage: `Neapolitan Pizza in Downtown Asheville | Rosa's Trattoria`

- Menu: `Menu & Prices | Rosa's Trattoria, Asheville NC`

- Location: `Find Us — Parking, Directions & Hours | Rosa's Trattoria`

- Events: `Private Dining & Events in Asheville | Rosa's Trattoria`

Lead with what people search, close with your name. Never leave two pages with the same title, and never let the CMS ship `Home | Home`.

## 5. Content that earns local rankings

Restaurants do not need a content marketing operation. They need a small number of pages that say specific, true, locally-grounded things. Quality and specificity beat volume every time.

### Write for the neighborhood, not the city

"Restaurants in Chicago" is unwinnable and, for you, nearly worthless. "Where to eat near the Music Box Theatre" is winnable, converts far better, and is exactly the kind of thing people ask an AI assistant. Name your neighborhood, the cross streets, the theater, the stadium, the campus, the hotels, the transit stops. These are the words locals and visitors actually use.

### The Visit page

One page, and it should answer everything a person needs before getting in the car:

- Full address, embedded map, and what the building looks like from the street

- Parking — lot, street, garage, valet, what it costs, where to go if the lot is full

- Transit — nearest stops and lines, walking time

- Landmarks — "two blocks north of the courthouse", "across from the arena"

- Accessibility — entrance, restroom, seating, service animals

- What to expect — dress, noise, wait times, whether you take walk-ins

### Seasonal and event content that is worth the effort

Only when it is real and timely. A page about your Valentine's prix fixe published in January earns its keep. A generic post about "the history of pasta" does not. Tie content to things that are actually happening: menu changes, local festivals, game days, graduation weekend, restaurant week, holiday hours.

> **The reverse-search trick**
>
> Open Search Console, sort your queries by impressions, and read the ones where you show up but rarely get clicked. Those are questions people are asking that your site answers badly or not at all. Each one is a page or a section waiting to be written — and unlike keyword tools, this is your own real demand data.

## 6. Reviews as a system

Reviews are now doing three jobs at once: they rank you in the local pack, they persuade the human reading them, and they are the raw material an AI assistant summarizes when somebody asks whether your place is any good. That third job is new, and it changes the stakes. Your reviews have become your sales copy, written by strangers.

### What actually matters, in order

- **Velocity** — a steady flow of new reviews. Twenty reviews spread over ten months beats twenty in one week, which looks manufactured.

- **Volume** — relative to the restaurants ranking above you, not in the abstract.

- **Rating** — 4.3 to 4.8 is the sweet spot. A perfect 5.0 with 30 reviews reads as suspicious to both humans and machines.

- **Response rate** — replies are public text you control, and they measurably change how a negative review lands.

- **Content** — reviews that name specific dishes and occasions are worth more than "great food!", because they are what gets quoted.

### Building the ask into the shift

The restaurants that win at reviews are not running campaigns. They have made the ask routine: a QR code on the check presenter, a line in the receipt email, a link in the booking confirmation text, and servers who mention it naturally at a good moment. Five asks a shift is 150 a month, and that is a transformed profile within a quarter.

> **Three ways to get yourself penalized**
>
> Do not gate. Asking only happy customers for reviews — or screening people to a feedback form first and only the pleased ones to Google — violates platform policy and gets review sets wiped. Do not pay, in cash, discounts, free desserts or entries in a draw. Do not solicit on Yelp at all, which forbids asking entirely; there you improve by responding and by fixing what people complain about.

### Replying to a bad review

Structure that works, kept under 100 words:

- Thank them for the feedback and name the specific issue, so it is clear you read it.

- Apologize plainly, with no "sorry you feel that way" and no excuses.

- State the concrete thing you are changing, if there is one.

- Offer a direct way to continue offline — a real name and email.

Never argue, never blame the guest, never mention a comp in public. Remember your audience is not the reviewer; it is the next hundred people who read that review while deciding.

### Reviews as free operational data

Once a quarter, read your last hundred reviews and tally the recurring themes. It is the cheapest customer research you will ever get, and it doubles as an AEO diagnostic: whatever those themes are, that is roughly what an AI assistant is telling diners about you. If "slow service on weekends" appears eleven times, fix the weekend service — and know that until you do, that sentence is part of your description in every AI answer.

## 7. Structured data without a developer

Schema markup is a block of code in your page that spells out your facts in a format machines cannot misinterpret. Your page says "Open 5pm till late, Tues–Sun" and a human understands it. Schema says `opensAt 17:00, closesAt 23:00, dayOfWeek Tuesday` and there is nothing left to interpret.

For AEO this matters more than it ever did for SEO. Retrieval systems favour facts they can extract with confidence, and schema is the highest-confidence format available.

### What a restaurant needs

| Type | Goes on | What it declares | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Restaurant | Homepage | Name, address, geo, phone, hours, cuisine, price range, reservations, sameAs | Essential |
| Menu / MenuItem | Menu page | Sections, dishes, descriptions, prices, dietary suitability | High |
| FAQPage | FAQ page | Question and answer pairs | High |
| LocalBusiness hours | All pages | Special and holiday hours | Medium |
| Event | Events page | Date, time, price, booking link | Medium, if you host events |
| BreadcrumbList | All pages | Site structure | Low |

### The three-step process, no coding required

- Use the generator on this page, or describe your restaurant to an AI assistant and ask for schema.org Restaurant JSON-LD.

- Paste the result into Google's **Rich Results Test** at `search.google.com/test/rich-results`. If it reports errors, paste them back to the AI and ask for a fix.

- Add the validated block to the `<head>` of your page. Most platforms have a "custom code" or "header injection" field; on WordPress a schema plugin or the theme's header hook will do it.

> **Two rules that keep you out of trouble**
>
> Only mark up what is visible on the page. Schema describing content a visitor cannot see is a policy violation. Never mark up your own aggregate review rating. Self-serving review markup on your own site is against Google's guidelines and is a common cause of manual penalties — your Google profile handles ratings for you.

## 8. AEO: how AI actually picks a restaurant

When someone asks an assistant where to eat, roughly this happens: it works out what they actually want (cuisine, area, occasion, constraints), it retrieves information from sources it can reach — its own index, a live web search, maps and review data, third-party roundups — it decides which of those sources to trust, and it composes an answer naming a small number of places with a reason for each.

Every one of those steps is something you can influence.

| Step | What decides the outcome | What you do about it |
|---|---|---|
| Can it find you? | Indexed site, crawlers not blocked, claimed profiles, listed on aggregators | Check robots.txt and your CDN's bot rules; claim every profile |
| Can it read you? | Menu in text not PDF, facts in HTML, schema markup | Chapters 3 and 7 |
| Does it trust you? | Consistent facts across sources, reviews, third-party mentions, named people | Reconcile every listing; earn local press |
| Can it match you to the question? | Explicit facts about occasions, diets, capacity, parking, atmosphere | The FAQ and Visit pages |
| Will it quote you? | Answer-first structure, self-contained passages | Structure, below |

### Write so a machine can lift a paragraph out

Retrieval systems work with passages, not pages. The single most effective structural change you can make is this:

- Make the **question the heading** — "Do you take reservations for large groups?"

- **Answer completely in the first 40 to 60 words**, before any context or story.

- Make each answer **stand alone** — it should make sense lifted out of the page with no surrounding text.

- Put the detail, the caveats and the personality **after** the direct answer.

Compare. Weak: *"We're often asked about group bookings. Our dining room has a lovely history — it was a hardware store until 1987 — and over the years we've hosted…"* Strong: *"Yes. We take reservations for groups up to 12 in the main dining room and up to 30 in the private room downstairs. Book online or call at least 48 hours ahead. Groups of 8 or more have a set menu."* The second one gets quoted; the first one does not.

### State the facts a machine will never infer

This is where most restaurants lose. An assistant will not guess that you take reservations, that there is a parking garage next door, or that the kitchen can handle a celiac diner. If your site does not say it, the answer becomes "I'm not sure" and the diner moves on to a place that did say it. Write these down explicitly, somewhere on the site, in plain text:

- Price range and typical spend per person

- Reservation policy, how far ahead, and the largest party you seat

- Private dining capacity and what it costs

- Dietary accommodation — specifically, not just "we have options"

- Parking, transit and accessibility

- Kids, dress code, noise level, dogs on the patio

- Whether you do takeout, delivery, catering — and through whom

- Last seating and kitchen close, which differ from your posted hours

### Consistency is a trust signal

When your website says you close at 10, Google says 11 and Yelp says 9:30, an assistant has three conflicting facts and no way to resolve them. What it usually does is hedge — "hours vary, check ahead" — or leave you out in favour of somewhere unambiguous. Pick your website as the source of truth and reconcile everything else to it, every time something changes.

### Get onto the pages AI already trusts

Assistants lean heavily on third-party roundups: local publications, "best of" lists, tourism boards, aggregators. Ask Perplexity for the best restaurants in your city and look at what it cites — that citation list *is* your outreach target list. Getting included on a page an assistant already trusts is usually faster than becoming a trusted source yourself.

### Check whether you are blocking the crawlers

A surprising number of restaurants are invisible to AI assistants because a security plugin or CDN setting blocks the crawlers. Open `yourdomain.com/robots.txt` and look for these:

| Crawler | Who | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| OAI-SearchBot | OpenAI | Powers search results and citations in ChatGPT. Allow this one. |
| GPTBot | OpenAI | Collects data for model training. Optional — separate decision. |
| PerplexityBot | Perplexity | Indexes for Perplexity answers. Allow. |
| ClaudeBot | Anthropic | Crawls for Claude. Allow. |
| Google-Extended | Google | Gemini training. Does not affect normal Search or AI Overviews. |
| Bingbot | Microsoft | Feeds Bing and a share of Copilot answers. Always allow. |

Robots.txt is not the only place this happens — bot-protection rules at your CDN or host can block these regardless of what robots.txt says. If you are unsure, ask whoever manages your hosting to confirm these user agents are not being challenged.

### Measuring AEO

There is no Search Console for this. You measure it by asking. Build a fixed list of 10 to 15 realistic diner questions, run them across ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Copilot and Google AI Overviews on the same day each month, and record four things: whether you were mentioned, what position, whether the facts stated were correct, and which sources were cited. That is the whole methodology, and it works. There is a tracker template further down this page.

## 9. Working with AI without getting burned

AI is genuinely useful here, and it is also confidently wrong about local facts in ways that will cost you. The line is clean once you see it.

### ✅ Let AI do this

- Turning a photographed menu into HTML

- Writing dish descriptions from ingredients you supply

- Generating and debugging schema markup

- Drafting FAQ answers from your policies

- Three versions of a review reply while you are still annoyed

- Title tags and meta descriptions in bulk

- Summarizing 100 reviews into themes

- Translating a PageSpeed report into a work order

- A month of Google Posts from your specials calendar

- Telling you what facts your site is missing

### 🚫 Never let AI do this

- State your hours, prices or address without you checking

- Invent dishes, ingredients or allergen information

- Claim awards, press or credentials you do not have

- Write reviews, or reply to one without you reading it

- Publish anything about a health, allergen or dietary claim unverified

- Name local landmarks or businesses you have not confirmed exist

- Post to your Google profile unsupervised

- Produce a page of generic filler to "have content"

### What makes a prompt work

Five things, and the prompts in the library below all have them:

- **Role and context** — who you are, what restaurant, which city.

- **Real inputs** — your actual menu, your actual reviews, your actual policies. Generic in, generic out.

- **A specific task** — "write 8 Google Posts under 1,000 characters with a CTA", not "help me with marketing".

- **Required format** — a table, HTML, a numbered list, a character limit.

- **An anti-hallucination instruction** — "do not invent anything; mark what you are unsure about as [UNCLEAR]".

> **The allergen rule**
>
> Never publish AI-generated allergen or dietary information without your kitchen checking it line by line. An AI assistant will cheerfully describe a dish as gluten-free based on the name alone. That is a medical risk and a legal one, and it is the one place in this entire playbook where being fast is not worth it.

## 10. Multiple locations

Everything above still applies, once per location. What changes is the structure.

- **One Google Business Profile per physical location.** Never one profile for the group. Each needs its own hours, photos, posts and review responses.

- **One website page per location**, on a clean URL like `/locations/riverside/`. A single page listing all your addresses ranks for none of them.

- **Make the location pages genuinely different.** Different photos, different neighborhood context, different parking, different landmarks, the actual manager's name, location-specific specials. Near-duplicate location pages get filtered out of results.

- **Location-specific schema** on each page, with that location's address, geo coordinates and hours.

- **Route reviews to the right location** and reply from that location's profile.

- **Watch for duplicates after a move.** Old addresses linger in directories for years and quietly send people to a locked door.

## 11. What to ignore

Things that consume restaurant marketing budgets and return very little:

- **Buying backlinks or "1,000 directory submissions".** Low-quality link packages range from useless to actively harmful. Local relevance beats link volume.

- **Keyword stuffing your business name.** "Rosa's Trattoria - Best Italian Restaurant Asheville NC" in your Google profile name violates policy and gets reported by competitors. Use your real name.

- **Blogging for its own sake.** Nobody is reading "10 Fun Facts About Olive Oil". Spend that hour on your menu page instead.

- **Chasing city-wide head terms.** "Restaurants in Chicago" is not a realistic or even valuable target for one independent. Neighborhood and dish terms are.

- **Review gating and paid reviews.** Covered above. The downside is losing your entire review set.

- **Rebuilding a working website to fix SEO.** Almost always the fix is content, speed and schema — not a new theme.

- **Agencies that will not show you the Google profile first.** If local SEO is not the first conversation, it is not local SEO.

- **Anyone guaranteeing rankings.** Nobody controls Google's results, and the ones who claim to are usually using techniques that eventually cost you.

## 12. Measuring what matters

Rankings are a vanity number — they change by device, by location, by the hour. Measure things that connect to covers.

| Metric | Where | How often | What good looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direction requests | Google profile | Monthly | Trending up; strong intent to visit |
| Calls from the profile | Google profile | Monthly | Up, and answered |
| Website clicks from profile | Google profile | Monthly | Up alongside views |
| Discovery vs direct searches | Google profile | Monthly | Discovery growing = new people finding you |
| New reviews per month | Google profile | Weekly | Steady, never zero |
| Average rating | Google profile | Monthly | 4.3 to 4.8, stable or rising |
| Review response rate | Google profile | Weekly | 100% |
| Clicks and impressions | Search Console | Monthly | Up, especially non-brand queries |
| Queries in positions 8–20 | Search Console | Monthly | Your cheapest wins list |
| AI mention rate | Manual test | Monthly | Rising share of your 15 test questions |
| AI fact accuracy | Manual test | Monthly | Zero errors — chase every one to its source |
| Online orders / bookings | Your own system | Weekly | The number that actually pays wages |

> **Set expectations honestly**
>
> Google Business Profile changes can show up within days to a couple of weeks. Website content and schema typically take four to twelve weeks to settle. Reviews and local authority compound over months. AI assistant answers can lag further still, because some of them are working from indexes refreshed on their own schedule. Do the work, keep the monthly loop, and judge it on a quarter — not on a Tuesday.
